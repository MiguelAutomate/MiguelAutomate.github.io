Read firstThree things no skill on this page covers

Tapering

If what you're coming off includes benzodiazepines or alcohol, stopping fast can cause seizures and can be fatal. That taper needs a doctor setting the schedule — not willpower, and not someone holding the bottle for you.

After a break

If opioids are in the picture, tolerance drops fast. The amount that felt normal two weeks ago can be an overdose today. Naloxone in the house, and don't use alone.

An unsafe house

If someone you live with is a current source of harm, grounding helps you survive a moment — it doesn't make a room safe. That needs a plan, and one person who knows about it who doesn't live there.

01 · The map

The memory that arrives without being asked

It doesn't come as a thought you chose. It comes as a body event first — heart, breath, gut, heat, a sudden need to leave — and the words arrive afterwards, if they arrive at all. By the time you're thinking about it, your nervous system has been running for a few seconds already.

So the catch is aimed at the body, not the story. You're looking for the moment your chest changes, not the moment the memory finishes playing. That's the whole skill, and it's the only one worth practising this week.

Why the turn is chosen in advance: when you're flooded, the part of the brain that generates options is the part that's least available. Choosing between four things you already decided on is possible in that state. Inventing a plan is not.

Why prayer and singing are on that list

Not as consolation. Because of what they do to a flooded nervous system — and because they're already yours.

Singing out loud

Breath + verbal channel

It forces a long, controlled exhale, one of the few voluntary levers on the body's alarm system. It also takes the verbal channel rumination runs on — you can't silently rehearse a memory and sing words at the same time.

Prayer, aloud, fixed words

Structure + nothing to compose

Same mechanism, plus one more: a prayer you know by heart asks nothing of the part of you that's currently offline. Keep one as the one — the familiarity is doing real work.

You don't have to want it less. You have to get there earlier.

One thing worth saying plainly: a memory that arrives on its own isn't something you did, thought, or invited. It's an alarm going off, not a character flaw and not a failure of faith. What's yours is what you say back to it — that's this whole page, and that part is genuinely in your hands.

03 · The card

Six lines, in order

Screenshot this one
  1. Catch the body, not the story. Chest, breath, jaw, stomach. That's the signal — it comes before the words do.
  2. Say what it is, out loud. "That's the memory — it's not happening now." Name the room you're in.
  3. Turn — one of your four. Chosen on a calm day, not invented in the middle of this one.
  4. Ninety seconds, then twenty minutes. You're not making it stop. You're standing still while it passes. It does pass.
  5. Tell someone the same day. Good day or bad day. The secrecy is what makes the next one harder.
  6. Nothing about yourself — not even as a joke. Words are instructions. The punchline gets filed exactly like the sentence.
