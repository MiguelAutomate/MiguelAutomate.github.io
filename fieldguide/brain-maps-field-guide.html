The gap between the signal and the reach

Every map in here covers one short stretch of time: the seconds between something setting your nervous system off and your hand doing what it always does. That gap is real, it is short, and it can be widened with practice. None of these pages ask you to be stronger. They ask you to be earlier.

A companion, not a treatment plan · Keep it on your phone · Read one plate, not all seven

Read firstThree things that skill alone doesn't cover

Alcohol and benzodiazepines

If either is part of the picture, do not stop abruptly on your own. Those two withdrawals are the ones that can cause seizures and can be fatal — even at doses that felt ordinary.

The exit is a supervised taper. A clinician has to be in that loop.

Opioids after a break

Tolerance drops fast. The dose that felt normal two weeks ago can be an overdose today, which is why the risk spikes after a good stretch, not during a bad one.

Keep naloxone within reach, tell someone nearby where it is, and don't use alone.

An unsafe house isn't a coping problem

If someone you live with is a current source of harm, no breathing exercise fixes that. Grounding helps you survive a moment. It does not make a room safe.

That one needs a plan, and at least one person who knows about it who does not live there.

00 · How to read a plate

Three colours, one shape, every time

Every map reads straight down the screen, and they all have the same skeleton — so you only have to learn it once. The colours mean the same thing on all seven plates:

The pull

Plum · what's happening to you

Cues, cravings, memories arriving on their own, a phone lighting up at 11 p.m. You didn't choose any of this and you are not in trouble for it.

The catch

Green · the moment you notice

The most trainable step there is. Not resisting — noticing. Naming it out loud counts: "that's the craving," "that's the memory, not the room."

The redirect

Blue · what you do instead

Something with a body attached — sing, pray out loud, walk, wash, call. It has to occupy the same channel the pull is using, or it won't hold.

You don't have to want it less. You have to get there earlier.

One more thing about the shape. Every plate has a fork in it, and the fork is never use or don't use. It's always something smaller and earlier: pick up the phone or leave it, walk in the front door or sit in the car two minutes first, answer at 11 p.m. or let it ring. By the time the fork reads use or don't, the map has been running for twenty minutes. These plates are drawn to show the earlier forks, because those are the winnable ones.

01 · The battery

You hold a charge, and there are only three things to do with it

Think of yourself as a battery. Everything you do with a day either puts charge in, pulls charge out, or shorts the whole cell. Most choices aren't dramatic — they're small, and they're constant, and they all land in one of three columns.

Charge it

Positive · builds capacity

Positive choices, positive actions, positive prayer. Showing up somewhere you said you'd be. Doing something for a person who can't do it alone. Charge is built by actions, not moods — you don't wait to feel it first.

Drain it

Negative · leaks capacity

Talking down about yourself. Rooms you already know are bad. People who need you at your worst so they feel better at theirs. Nothing collapses here — it just quietly costs, all day.

Short it out

Killing it · zero capacity

The substance, the all-nighter, the secret. Not a slow drain — a dead cell. And a dead cell can't make a good decision about anything else on this page, which is why this one comes first.

Words are energy. Said out loud, they're instructions — and something is always listening.

The power of words

No jokes

The punchline isn't filed separately

Joking down on yourself programs your mind exactly like a serious remark does. "I'm such a mess, lol" installs the same line as "I'm a mess." Everyone laughs and it still goes in.

Words are instructions

Spoken beats thought

What you say out loud is the loudest input your own nervous system gets all day. That's why the redirects in here are spoken ones — a prayer said aloud, a song sung out. Same lever, pointed the other way.

What you won't change, you've chosen

Silence is a decision too

A habit you refuse to look at is a habit you've agreed to keep. Not looking isn't neutral; it's the quietest way of saying yes.

Two exceptions worth knowing So this never turns into a stick to hit yourself with
A memory that arrives on its own is not something you said

You didn't speak it, invite it, or cause it by thinking wrong. Intrusive memories are the alarm going off, not self-talk. Your words are what you say back to it — that's Plate III, and that's the part that's yours.

Some sentences in your head were installed by someone else

A line an adult said about you once — especially when you were young, especially one that was protecting them and not you — can keep running for years as if it were your own verdict. It isn't yours. You're allowed to hand it back and stop repeating it for them.

The distinction that matters: you are responsible for how you speak about yourself. You are not responsible for what got done to you, or for the alarm your body still runs because of it. Those are two different files, and shame keeps trying to merge them.

Plate I

The loop that runs before you decide anything

This is the general engine. Everything else here is one specific version of it. The claim on this plate: the reach is not a decision you keep losing. It's a route driven so many times that it stopped asking. Which is why trying harder at the end of the loop keeps failing, and trying earlier works.

Plate II

The wave, and the ninety seconds in the middle of it

A craving isn't a straight line that climbs until you give in. It's a wave: it rises, it crests, and if nothing feeds it, it comes down on its own — usually inside twenty to thirty minutes. The trap is that the crest feels permanent while you're standing in it.

The first minute or two is the part where doing nothing feels physically impossible. That's what the timer is for. Ninety seconds isn't the whole craving — it's the length of the part you have to get past before your own reasoning comes back online.

1:30

Ride it out — you don't have to do anything else

Breathe out longer than you breathe in. Count the exhale if it helps. You're not trying to make the wave stop. You're standing still while it passes.

Plate III

The memory that arrives without being asked

This is the plate that matters most, so it's the longest. A traumatic memory doesn't arrive as a thought you chose. It arrives as a body event — heart, breath, gut, heat, a sudden urge to leave — and the words come afterward, if at all. By the time you're thinking about it, the nervous system has already been running for a few seconds.

So the catch has to be aimed at the body signal, not the story. You're looking for the moment your chest changes, not the moment the memory finishes playing.

Two honest notes. Redirecting gets you through a moment safely — it is not the same as processing what happened, and isn't meant to be; memories that keep arriving uninvited respond to real treatment. And this plate assumes the danger is in the memory. If the danger is also in the house right now, the panel at the top of this guide is the relevant page.

Why the turn is picked in advance: when the nervous system is flooded, the part of the brain that generates options is the part that's least available. Choosing between four things you already decided on is possible in that state. Inventing a plan is not. Write the four down; don't rely on remembering them.

Why prayer and singing work here, mechanically

These aren't consolation prizes on the list. They're on it because of how they work on a flooded nervous system — and because they're already hers.

Singing out loud

Breath + verbal channel

Singing forces a long, controlled exhale, one of the few voluntary levers on the body's alarm system. Slower out-breaths pull the physical response down.

It also occupies the verbal working memory that rumination runs on. You can't silently rehearse a memory and sing words at once — there's one channel, and the song has it.

Prayer, aloud, fixed wording

Structure + no composing required

Same mechanism, plus one more: a prayer you know by heart needs nothing from the part of you that's currently offline. No decisions, no words to find.

Pick one and keep it as the one. Familiarity is doing real work — it runs without you having to build it.

Hands and steps

Occupies the picture-making part

Intrusive memories arrive largely as images. Tasks demanding visual and spatial attention compete for that same capacity — which is why washing dishes or walking a known route dulls a memory while sitting still sharpens it.

The finger snap

A marker, not a cure

A snap is punctuation: a sharp, present-tense sound that says caught it and starts the sequence. It works because of what follows it, so always follow it with the actual turn. On its own it fades.

Plate V

The vape is a repetition problem, not a willpower problem

Nicotine is unusual: small dose, fast delivery, repeated dozens or hundreds of times a day. That's a lot of reps, and reps are exactly how a cue gets welded to an action. It also means the vape has attached itself to everything — the car, the coffee, the phone, the step outside, the pause between two tasks.

So this one responds better to changing the environment than to resisting in the moment. Distance beats discipline, because discipline has to win a hundred times a day and distance only has to be set up once.

Plate VI

After a slip: the fork that decides what it costs

A slip is one episode. What turns one episode into three weeks is almost never the substance — it's the sentence that comes next. I've ruined it. I'm exactly what I thought I was. Might as well finish the night. That sentence does more damage than the slip did, and it's the single most changeable thing on any of these plates.

Plate VIII

The pathway is strong — that's the good news, not the bad

Think of the brain as an engine. The part that runs the memory every day was built by bad engineering — nobody consulted you about it — but it was still built, by repetition, the same way every other route in there was built. Which means the machinery works fine. It's the destination that's wrong.

A route that strong took thousands of trips to lay down. That's usually said as bad news. Read it the other way: you already have proof that repetition builds something permanent in you. The only open question is what you point it at.

09 · The redirect menu

Pick four. Write them down. Don't add a fifth.

A redirect only works if it's decided in advance and it occupies the same channel the pull is using. Four is the right number — enough that one is always available, few enough to remember while flooded.

Sing it

Breath · verbal channel · already yours

Out loud, standing, the loud part. In the car, in the shower, at the sink. Choir or worship night counts double — it also fills an evening.

Pray it

Fixed words · no composing required

The same prayer every time, said aloud, short enough to finish. The last line can be the handoff into whatever you do next.

Move rooms, or leave the building

Changes the cue field entirely

A craving is partly made of where you're standing. Walking to a known destination and back beats sitting still and being strong.

Hands into a task with steps

Occupies the picture-making capacity

Dishes, sweeping, laundry, braiding, cooking one specific thing. Order matters more than scale — the steps do the work.

Call the person on the list

Breaks the secrecy the loop needs

Chosen while calm, told in advance they're on the list. "Just talk to me for ten minutes" is a complete sentence.

Go where you're expected — and take someone

Two jobs at once

A service, a Sunday school, a standing morning with family. It fills the hour that would otherwise be empty, and it puts you somewhere people notice whether you came.

Bringing someone who can't get there without you does something extra: it makes the trip about them, on the days it can't be about you.

Write one line about it

Turns a flood into information

Time, what happened just before, what the body did. A week of one-liners shows patterns invisible from inside any single day.

The redirect isn't a distraction. It's the thing that gets to happen instead.

    12 · Pocket card

    If you only keep one screen

    Screenshot this · six lines · in order
    1. Catch the body, not the story. Chest, breath, jaw, stomach. That's the signal, and it comes before the words do.
    2. Say what it is, out loud. "That's the craving." "That's the memory — it's not happening now." Name the room you're in.
    3. Turn — one of your four. Sing · pray · move · hands. Chosen on a calm day, not invented in the middle of this one.
    4. Ninety seconds, then twenty minutes. You're not making it stop. You're standing still while it passes. It does pass.
    5. Tell someone the same day. Good day or bad day. The secrecy is the part that makes the next one harder.
    6. Don't say it about yourself — not even as a joke. Words are instructions. The punchline gets filed the same as the sentence. Charge it, don't drain it.

    Where the ideas come from

    None of this was invented for this document. If any of it is worth chasing further:

    • The three-stage addiction cycle — Koob & Volkow's model of binge, withdrawal and preoccupation, and the shift from wanting to needing.
    • Relapse prevention and the abstinence violation effect — Marlatt and colleagues, on why the interpretation of a slip predicts what happens next.
    • Urge surfing — from mindfulness-based relapse prevention: cravings as waves that crest and decay when unreinforced.
    • Dual-task interference and intrusive memories — Holmes and colleagues, on visual-spatial tasks reducing the vividness and frequency of intrusive images.
    • Slow exhalation and autonomic state — the physiology behind why singing and spoken prayer settle the body faster than silent effort.
    • Trauma-focused treatment — for memories that keep arriving uninvited there are therapies with strong evidence behind them. This guide isn't one of them, and isn't meant to be a substitute.

    14 · For your person

    The page for whoever is walking this with you

    Hand this section to them. It's short on purpose, and it's the part most guides leave out — the people helping usually get no instructions at all, and then get blamed when instinct turns out to be wrong.

    After a slip, say

    "Thanks for telling me today."

    Rewards the disclosure, not the outcome

    Whether she tells you about the next one is being decided in this exact sentence. Lead with this before anything else, even if you're gutted.

    "What was happening right before?"

    Turns shame into data

    A real question with a real answer. It puts you both on the same side of the problem instead of across from each other.

    "What do you want to do in the next hour?"

    The only timeframe that matters

    Not tomorrow, not "from now on". Promises about the future are how the conversation escapes the room without anything changing.

    Don't say

    • "I thought you were doing so well." Turns her honesty into your disappointment, and teaches her that telling you costs more than hiding.
    • "You promised." Relitigating a promise mid-crisis has never once produced a different outcome.
    • Counting her days for her. If the streak becomes the currency between you, a slip stops being one night and becomes the loss of the whole relationship.
    • Nothing at all, with a hurt face. Silence gets read as disgust, and she'll fill it in with the worst version.

    When she's angry at you for caring

    It happens, it's common, and it isn't really about you. Don't argue and don't disappear — the leaving is what confirms the fear underneath it. "I'm not going anywhere. I'll be here when you want to talk" is a complete response, and you can say it and then genuinely go do something else.

    Your own limits, said out loud

    You can't be the friend, the coach, the accountability, and the emergency contact all at once — those roles conflict eventually, and when they do she loses all of them in one go. Say what you're not able to be. Then help her get a second person who isn't you: a counselor, a sponsor, someone from church, anyone whose only job is her.

    If you think it's an overdose Don't wait to be sure
    Signs

    Can't be woken · breathing slow, gurgling or stopped · lips or fingertips going blue or grey · limp body · skin cold and clammy.

    Call emergency services first

    Most US states have Good Samaritan laws that protect the caller. Say the person is unresponsive and not breathing normally.

    Give naloxone if you have it

    It does nothing if opioids aren't involved, so give it when in doubt. A second dose may be needed after a few minutes.

    On their side, and stay

    Recovery position so they can't choke. Stay until help arrives — naloxone wears off before the opioid does.

    Get naloxone before you need it. Most US pharmacies sell it without a prescription, and many health departments give it out free. Keep it where someone else could find it, and tell them where it is.
